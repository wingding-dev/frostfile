# Identity-theft protection services: what the evidence actually shows

Researched 2026-08-08 from primary sources — GAO-17-254, GAO-19-230, FTC and
CFPB guidance, IDShield's own plan documents, and an American Bankers/Assurant
Summary of Benefits.

Written up because it is the strongest available evidence for why FrostFile
exists, and because the fair version of the argument is more persuasive than
the cynical one. **Monitoring is not a scam. It is a smoke detector being sold
as a lock.**

---

## The core distinction, in the auditors' words

GAO-19-230:

> **A credit freeze is the only consumer option that can prevent one type of
> identity theft-related fraud**… In contrast, identity theft services and
> self-monitoring detect or remediate identity theft after it has occurred,
> **but do not prevent the fraud from occurring in the first place.**

The FTC's page states a freeze's cost as **"Free"**, that "nobody can open a
new credit account in your name," and that it "lasts until you lift it" and
"doesn't affect your credit score." Free freezes became federal law on
21 September 2018 (Pub. L. 115-174 § 301, at 15 U.S.C. § 1681c-1(i)) — before
that, consumers typically paid $5–$10 per agency.

**Freezes are not locks.** CFPB: "Don't confuse freezes with locks. They work
in a similar way, but locks may have monthly fees. If you want a free freeze
guaranteed by federal law, then opt for a freeze, not a lock."

And the uptake number that explains why this product needs to exist: the CBO
estimated roughly **0.3% of Americans with credit reports** had frozen them.

## The "$1 million protection", accurately

**It is expense reimbursement with narrow stolen-funds sublimits — not
indemnity.** GAO-17-254:

> **Identity theft insurance is limited in covering direct financial losses —
> that is, money that has been stolen.** Instead, the insurance generally
> reimburses consumers for out-of-pocket expenses they incur related to the
> process of restoring their identity.

Modern policies do include some stolen-funds coverage, but the construction
matters. IDShield's 2024 employer plan document lists cash recovery for
unauthorized electronic fund transfers and losses from financial accounts, each
"subject to a $1 million sublimit", with the footnote: **"This coverage is part
of and not in addition to the Expense Reimbursement."** The sublimits share one
aggregate; they do not stack.

Market-wide, GAO found the headline sat atop sublimits one to two orders of
magnitude smaller: **lost wages usually capped at $4,000–$7,500** and **stolen
funds from fraudulent transfers at $10,000–$50,000**.

Professional fees are capped at **$125/hour** for attorneys, private
investigators and CPAs — below prevailing consumer-litigation rates in most
metros, with anything above it expressly excluded.

**Why the cash-recovery benefit is thinner than it sounds:** Regulation E and
card-network zero-liability rules already cap consumer liability for
unauthorized transfers. The policy pays only the residue the bank didn't, and
requires you to seek reimbursement from the bank first.

**The exclusions that matter most for a post-breach free year:** loss
"discovered prior to the effective date of the Policy" and "any loss which
occurred while the member was not an active member." Your SSN and date of birth
never expire. **A one-year offer covers a twelve-month slice of a permanent
exposure**, and GAO notes plainly that "bad actors can use stolen information
for years after a breach."

And the gap nothing closes, per Gartner's Avivah Litan: "if someone takes out a
mortgage in your name and now you owe the bank $100k or more — nobody covers
that."

### Two things that are fair to the vendors

**Payouts go to the consumer.** The policy language is "We will reimburse the
member." There is no third-party loss payee, and the provider's own restoration
labor does not appear to draw against the limit — the $125/hour lines are for
outside professionals the member retains. The common suspicion that the vendor
pockets the insurance is not supported.

**IDShield's restoration is real work.** Its licensed investigators take a
Limited Power of Attorney to act with bureaus, creditors, SSA, FTC and USPS —
more than most competitors provide. Its plan documents also cover
*pre-existing* identity theft restoration as a service, even though the
insurance excludes pre-existing losses.

## How often it pays: essentially never, and disclosure is getting worse

GAO asked providers directly. Claims "appear to rarely exceed a few thousand
dollars":

- A market-leading insurer: successful claims **averaged $500–$1,500**; the
  **largest ever paid was about $50,000**
- One provider: **"zero or 'almost zero' claims in recent years"**
- One provider: a **loss ratio under 2%** — for comparison, ordinary
  property-casualty lines run 50–70%
- One provider with a government contract covering **4+ million people** had
  paid **one claim, for $1,519**

**The providers admitted why the number is a million.** GAO, verbatim:

> Some identity theft service providers with whom we spoke **acknowledged that
> identity theft insurance is of limited value to a consumer and that it was
> hard to imagine covered losses approaching the $1 million limit.** Two
> providers told us that **the primary reason they provided identity theft
> insurance — and in an amount of $1 million — was to stay competitive.**

The reason claims are so rare is structural: per BJS, only **5% of identity
theft victims report any indirect losses at all**, and among those who do the
**median is $10**. The policy reimburses a category of cost that is zero for
95% of victims.

**Disclosure has since regressed.** The NAIC's Cybersecurity and Identity Theft
Insurance Coverage Supplement was the one mandatory filing capturing claims and
payouts. Since 2024 it "no longer requests data pertaining to identity theft
specifically." The decade-old GAO figures remain the best public data.

**And there is no evidence any of it works.** GAO-19-230 "did not identify any
studies" showing that purchasers of these services suffered less identity theft
or detected fraud faster than people monitoring their own accounts for free.

## Giving monitoring its due — what a freeze does not cover

A freeze argument that skips this is dishonest, and users will hit these gaps.

| Threat | Freeze | Monitoring |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New-account credit fraud | **Prevents** | Detects only |
| Existing-account fraud | No | Bank's own alerts are the control — and this is *most* identity theft |
| Tax-refund fraud | No | **No service addresses it.** The IRS IP PIN is the only answer |
| Medical identity theft | No | Only 1 of 26 services GAO reviewed. Victims may be *liable*, average ~$13,450 |
| Employment / SSN-for-work | No | Partial. E-Verify Self Lock is the real control |
| Government benefits fraud | No | Largely not. SSA's direct-deposit block is the control |
| Synthetic identity | Weak — may never touch your file | Some |
| Criminal identity theft | No | **Court-records monitoring genuinely addresses this** |
| Address-change / mail redirect | No | **NCOA monitoring catches this** |
| Dark-web exposure | No | Signal only; the data cannot be un-leaked |

A freeze also does **not** block: companies you already have accounts with (and
their collection agencies), insurance underwriters, employment/tenant/background
screeners, courts and government agencies in some circumstances, and
pre-approved credit offers — which is why `optoutprescreen.com` matters.

**The fair conclusion: the error is not buying monitoring. The error is
substituting the smoke detector for the lock.**

## Post-breach offers as a compliance and PR instrument

GAO, under its own heading "Liability mitigation":

> Some companies may offer identity theft services **to protect themselves from
> lawsuits.** A 2014 study of data breach litigation concluded that **lawsuits
> were less likely when the breached company offered credit monitoring.**

GAO also found breached companies "generally do not assess vendor
effectiveness" when choosing one — their criteria are price, reputation,
capacity, and notification compliance. **Efficacy is not on the list.**

But the honest version is more complicated than "it's a shield." Several
circuits have used the offer as evidence *supporting* plaintiffs' standing
(*Remijas v. Neiman Marcus*, 7th Cir. 2015; *Galaria v. Nationwide*, 6th Cir.
2016), while others refused to (*Beck v. McDonald*, 4th Cir. 2017). Defense
firms publish alerts warning that the offer can backfire. It is a
compliance-and-PR instrument whose litigation effect is genuinely uncertain.

Some states simply mandate it: **Connecticut ≥24 months**, **Massachusetts 18
months** (42 if the breached entity is itself a credit bureau), **Delaware 1
year** where an SSN is involved. California requires 12 months only *if* an
offer is made.

### Does enrolling waive anything?

**No documented case exists** in which enrolling in a breached company's free
monitoring waived or defeated a claim against it. Three layers get conflated:

1. **The employer's offer.** Massachusetts expressly forbids conditioning it on
   a waiver — legislators thought the risk real enough to legislate against.
2. **The vendor's own terms.** This is where arbitration clauses live. The 2017
   Equifax episode is the case study: TrustedID Premier's terms carried an
   arbitration clause and class-action waiver broad enough that a state AG and
   ten senators intervened; Equifax denied it ever covered breach claims, then
   removed it. No court ever ruled. **Equifax was uniquely alarming because the
   breached party and the monitoring vendor were the same company** — not the
   usual situation.
3. **Class-action settlements.** These genuinely do release claims — but you are
   bound **because you failed to opt out**, not because you filed a claim.
   Declining to claim while also declining to opt out leaves you with the
   release and nothing else. That is the default outcome for most class members.

## What to actually tell someone after an SSN breach

Take the free year — it costs nothing and its court-records, address-change and
financial-account monitoring cover real ground. Then do the things that
actually protect you, all free and none of which expire:

1. **myE-Verify Self Lock first** — enrollment uses an Experian
   knowledge-based quiz that a credit freeze can block.
2. **IRS IP PIN** — the only control for tax-refund fraud. The tool is open
   only mid-January to mid-November.
3. **Freeze** the three nationwide bureaus, then Innovis, NCTUE, ChexSystems,
   LexisNexis/SageStream, Clarity, DataX and FactorTrust.
4. **Claim your accounts before an attacker does** — SSA, USPS Informed
   Delivery. The knowledge-based questions guarding them are answerable *from
   the data that leaked*.
5. **Call SSA at 1-800-772-1213** for both blocks — especially Direct Deposit
   Fraud Prevention, which closes the bank auto-enrollment path.
6. **Free carrier SIM/port locks** — the FCC's notification rule is *not* in
   force.
7. **optoutprescreen.com**, a fraud alert, and calendar the free weekly reports.

**One question worth asking the employer:** at OPM, insurance and restoration
were available to the entire affected population *whether or not they
enrolled* — only monitoring required signing up. If the same is true here, the
enrollment decision is narrower than it looks and the coverage may already
attach without accepting any terms.

**And calendar the expiry date.** Krebs: these services "typically hard-sell
consumers to sign up for paid credit protection plans when the free coverage
expires." There are 39,409 CFPB complaints on file about problems cancelling
credit monitoring.
