The big misunderstanding

Your credit cards keep working.

Almost everyone hears “freeze your credit” and pictures their cards going dead. They don't. A freeze doesn't touch anything you already have — cards, loans, bank accounts all work exactly as before, and your credit score is untouched[1]. It blocks one thing: someone opening a new account in your name. And unlocking for your own new loan is free and takes minutes. The app's Learn page walks through all of it, with sources.

Worth knowing

Those security questions aren’t secret any more.

When a site asks which street you lived on in 2009, or what your car payment was, it’s checking your answer against your credit file. That worked when you were the only one who knew. After a breach you’re not — your old addresses, your birthday and your Social Security number are all in the file somebody bought. The federal standard for proving who you are now says plainly that these questions must not use information available “freely, for a fee in the public domain, or via the black market”[1] — which is exactly where a breach puts it.

So there’s a quiet race on, and it’s an easy one to win once you know about it. Setting up your own accounts at the IRS, Social Security and the Postal Service matters as much as the freezes do, because once you’ve claimed yours, a stranger can’t claim it as you. FrostFile lists those sign-ups next to the freezes, with the link for each and what it blocks.

Why it's free

The valuable half was always free. Nobody just told you.

Paid identity services bundle two things. One is real-time alerts from your credit file — and there's no consumer version of that at any price, because federal law restricts who can pull a report. Nothing you could buy or build replicates it.

The other half is simply knowing which fifteen places will let you lock your file, and keeping track of what you've done. That part is free, entirely yours, and nobody does it well.

FrostFile does that half — and asks nothing back. No account, no data to hand over, no subscription to cancel.

Why is the download 30–75 MB?

At heart it's a checklist — but it ships with everything it needs baked in, including its own private window engine and runtime. That means nothing to install, no extra pieces, and nothing fetched from the internet, ever. The size is the self-contained privacy.

My browser or antivirus warned me. Is this safe?

Here's the honest version. Browsers and antivirus programs judge new software by reputation: has it been downloaded a million times, and did a company pay for a publisher certificate? FrostFile is weeks old and free, so it has neither — and warnings on brand-new, uncertified software are those tools doing their job. It is not a virus detection; it's a "we don't know this one yet."

What you don't have to take on faith: the complete source code is public at github.com/wingding-dev/frostfile, every download's fingerprint is published in SHA256SUMS.txt so anyone can verify their copy is untampered, and the program makes zero internet connections — which you can prove yourself by turning off Wi-Fi and watching everything still work. If a warning appears, "Keep" / "More info → Run anyway" is you telling your computer you trust the person who gave you this. If you don't know who that is, don't run it — that rule protects you everywhere, not just here.
